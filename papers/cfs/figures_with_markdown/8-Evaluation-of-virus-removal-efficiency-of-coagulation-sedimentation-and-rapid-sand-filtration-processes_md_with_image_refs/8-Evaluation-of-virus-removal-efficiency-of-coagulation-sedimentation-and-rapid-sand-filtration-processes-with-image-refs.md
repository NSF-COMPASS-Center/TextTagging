In order to properly assess and manage the risk of infection by enteric viruses in tap water, virus removal ef /uniFB01 ciency should be evaluated quantitatively for individual processes in actual drinking water treatment plants (DWTPs); however, there have been only a few studies due to technical dif /uniFB01 culties in quantifying low virus concentration in water samples. In this study, the removal ef /uniFB01 ciency of indigenous viruses was evaluated for coagulation-sedimentation (CS) and rapid sand /uniFB01 ltration (RSF) processes in a DWTP in Bangkok, Thailand by measuring the concentration of viruses before and after treatment processes using real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR). Water samples were collected and concentrated from raw source water, after CS, and after RSF, and inhibitory substances in water samples were reduced by use of a hydrophobic resin (DAX-8). Pepper mild mottle virus (PMMoV) and JC polyomavirus (JC PyV) were found to be highly prevalent in raw waters, with concentrations of 10 2.88 ± 0.35 and 10 3.06 ± 0.42 copies/L (geometric mean ± S.D.), respectively. Step-wise removal ef /uniFB01 ciencies were calculated for individual processes, with some variation observed between wet and dry seasons. During the wet season, PMMoV was removed less by CS and more by RSF on average (0.40 log10 vs 1.26 log10, respectively), while the reverse was true for JC PyV (1.91 log10 vs 0.49 log10, respectively). Both viruses were removed similarly during the dry season, with CS removing the most virus (PMMoV, 1.61 log10 and 0.78 log10; JC PyV, 1.70 log10, and 0.59 log10; CS and RSF, respectively). These differences between seasons were potentially due to variations in raw water quality and the characteristics of the viruses themselves. These results suggest that PMMoV and JC PyV, which are more prevalent in environmental waters than the other enteric viruses evaluated in this study, could be useful in determining viral fate for the risk management of viruses in water treatment processes in actual full-scale DWTPs.

## 1. Introduction

Viral gastroenteritis can be caused by enteric viruses (such as norovirus) via waterborne routes. These viruses replicate in the intestinal tract of the infected individual and are then excreted with feces in high concentrations. Enteric viruses have been detected in rivers, reservoirs, and groundwaters that are utilized as sources of drinking water (Chigor and Okoh, 2012; Lodder et al., 2010; Sibanda

and Okoh, 2013; Steyer et al., 2011; Ye et al., 2012). Normally, the concentrations of virus are low and are suf /uniFB01 ciently treated by drinking water treatment plants (DWTPs); however, in the event of a lapse in one or more of the water treatment processes, it is possible that a large population could be exposed to enteric viruses via tap water. For example, Kukkula &amp; Maunula (1999) reported a tap water-associated norovirus outbreak with an estimated 1700 e 3000 cases that was caused by inadequate treatment at a DWTP.

Information collected from raw water monitoring combined with treatment ef /uniFB01 ciencies evaluated for a given DWTP can be used to estimate the /uniFB01 nal concentration of viruses in the treated water. A quantitative microbial risk assessment (QMRA) can then be performed with these estimated concentrations and necessary strategies/technologies to control the risk can be suggested. This scheme of microbial risk management is currently practiced in the USA and the Netherlands (USEPA, 2006; Smeets et al., 2009).

Management of drinking water quality based on the concept of HACCP (Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Point) is recommended by the WHO (World Health Organization) through the Water Safety Plan (WHO, 2011), where it is recommended to set critical control points from the water source to the tap for controlling risk. The water treatment processes themselves are the main microbial control points in a DWTP, therefore they are a natural choice for critical control points in the risk management of drinking water. Generally, these treatment processes can be divided into physico-chemical particle removal processes and chemical disinfection processes. Chemical disinfection typically acts as the /uniFB01 nal barrier against waterborne viruses and plays a critical role in ensuring the safety of the supplied water; however, the evaluation of physical particle removal processes may be equally if not more important from the viewpoint of risk management. One reason for this idea is that disinfection processes will usually reduce already low concentrations of viruses to undetectable levels in the treated water, therefore the true disinfection ef /uniFB01 ciency of the system is dif /uniFB01 cult to ascertain. While disinfection ef /uniFB01 ciency can be modeled for various viruses, knowledge of the virus concentration prior to disinfection is essential for tailoring disinfection parameters (chlorine and O3 concentrations, contact times, etc.). For this reason, relying on only disinfection processes can increase the uncertainty of the risk. For example, different viruses can have varying tolerances to disinfection processes (Cromeans et al., 2010) leading to an unknown viral concentration in the treated water. Monitoring the concentration of infectious viruses after disinfection is also made dif /uniFB01 cult by the fact that cell culture methods typically used to evaluate virus inactivation have not been established for some relevant enteric viruses (e.g. human noroviruses) despite great efforts (Duizer et al., 2004; Jones et al., 2014). In addition, while more rapid monitoring of virus disinfection by qPCR is possible, the results of this can sometimes be misleading as PCR based methods cannot distinguish between intact viruses and inactivated viruses. It is possible, however, that the monitoring of virus removal during physical removal processes using qPCR before disinfection could give a more accurate representation of risk reduction from the treated water. If physical reduction rates could be determined for a given treatment system, plant operators would be better informed as to the actual concentration of viruses being subjected to disinfection, allowing them to optimize subsequent disinfection processes parameters for reducing viral loads to safe levels.

Among physico-chemical processes, coagulation-sedimentation (CS) and rapid sand /uniFB01 ltration (RSF) have been widely adopted around the world for drinking water treatment. Several studies have been conducted to determine the virus removal ef /uniFB01 ciency of these processes at a bench- or pilot plant-scale (Abbaszadegan et al., 2007, 2008; Gerba et al., 2003; Shin and Sobsey, 2015); however, few studies have determined virus removal ef /uniFB01 ciency at an actual full plant scale (Albinana-Gimenez et al., 2009; Ali et al., 2004; Gibson, 2014). This is likely due to the fact that the concentrations of indigenous viruses are usually too low to detect by practical methods in actual DWTPs. While bench-scale studies have the advantage of providing direct control over spiked virus concentrations and other system parameters, virus removal can be affected by errors in experiment design that do not re /uniFB02 ect operation conditions in actual plants. For example, virus removal in a bench-scale coagulation/sedimentation experiment may be easily impacted by minor disturbances of the coagulated material in a small container, whereas removal ef /uniFB01 ciencies measured from larger-scale processes would be less prone to these errors. In addition, other factors at large-scale plants such as /uniFB02 uctuating raw water quality and subsequent plant optimization of operational parameters could potentially impact virus removal; hence, it is essential to evaluate virus removal at full scale DWTPs in operation to properly assess the actual microbial risk.

While the monitoring of most enteric viruses in large-scale operations can be inef /uniFB01 cient due to the time and resources required for large volume sampling to detect them at such low concentrations, other viruses in the source water can be present in greater numbers and represent more reasonable targets for accessing virus removal ef /uniFB01 ciency. Recently, a number of viruses have been reported to be highly prevalent in water environments, including pepper mild mottle virus (PMMoV) (Betancourt et al., 2014; Hamza et al., 2011; Haramoto et al., 2013; Kuroda et al., 2015; Rosario et al., 2009), Aichi virus (Kitajima et al., 2011; Hata et al., 2014), adenovirus (Albinana-Gimenez et al., 2009; Aslan et al., 2011; Bo /uniFB01 ll-Mas et al., 2006; Fong et al., 2010; Hewitt et al., 2013) and (JC) polyomavirus (Albinana-Gimenez et al., 2009; Bo /uniFB01 llMas et al., 2006; Hewitt et al., 2013; Liang et al., 2015). The concentrations of these viruses may be high enough to evaluate their removal ef /uniFB01 ciency for different, in-series treatment processes of the DWTP.

The aim of this study was to evaluate the virus removal ef /uniFB01 -ciency of CS and RSF processes in an actual DWTP while taking seasonal variations into account. For this purpose, large volume water samples (0.5 e 450 L) were collected before and after CS and RSF at a DWTP in Bangkok, Thailand. Samples were collected over a three month period (August e November) encompassing the transition between the rainy and dry seasons in Thailand (mid-October) in order to determine the impacts, if any, of changing raw water quality on DWTP process ef /uniFB01 ciency. Viruses in these water samples were concentrated and quanti /uniFB01 ed by (RT) qPCR. PMMoV, Aichi virus, and JC/BK polyomavirus were targeted in addition to the main enteric viruses of concern for risk management, such as norovirus (GI and GII) and enteric adenovirus. Enteroviruses, a group of wellstudied water-borne viruses, were also targeted.

## 2. Materials and methods

## 2.1. Sample collection and water physico-chemical parameters

Water samples were collected from August to November of 2013 from the Bangkhen Water Treatment Plant in Bangkok, Thailand. This DWTP is one of the largest in the world, supplying approximately 3.6 /C2 10 6 m 3 of water per day to Bangkok city. The raw water is diverted from the Chao Phraya River at a point 18 km north (upstream) of the DWTP and then transported via canal. The water from the canal is used solely by the DWTP and is protected from sources of pollution such as wastewater discharge and surface runoff. The process /uniFB02 ow of the DWTP and main operation parameters are shown in Fig. 1. The treatment process of this DWTP consists of coagulation-sedimentation (CS), rapid sand /uniFB01 ltration (RSF), and chlorination, in that order. During the study period, aluminum sulfate was used as the coagulant at a dose of 45 e 60 mg/ L. The composition of the RSF ponds consisted of 80 cm of anthracite placed as the upper layer, and 40 cm of sand placed as the bottom layer. The /uniFB01 ltration rate of the RSF during the study period was 290 e 470 m/d.

In order to determine the division between wet and dry seasons, precipitation data was desired for this study; however, this was not possible due to the lack of high resolution rain data for the sampling area and uncertainty of which weather stations would provide the most accurate data with regards to changing river/canal water conditions. ## 2.2. Quanti /uniFB01 cation of indicator bacteria

E. coli and total coliforms were enumerated for all water samples using /uniFB01 lter units (37 mm MONITOR UNIT, 37 mm diameter, 0.45 m m pore size, ADVANTEC) wetted with m-ColiBlue24 ® Broth (HACH), a selective chromogenic culture media. Within 6 h of sample collection, a volume of 1 e 50 mL of the samples (depending on the sampling location) was passed through the /uniFB01 lters units to capture the bacteria on the /uniFB01 lter surface, after which a premeasured ampoule of the culture medium was added to the /uniFB01 lter unit. The /uniFB01 lters were incubated at 37 /C14 C overnight and then enumerated for E. coli (appearing as blue colonies) and total coliforms (the sum of red and blue colonies).

## 2.3. Concentration of viruses in water samples

Viruses in the water samples were concentrated within 24 h of collection by one of two adsorption-elution methods using electronegative membrane /uniFB01 lters, depending on the sample volume. Raw water samples were concentrated based on the method described by Katayama et al. (2002a, 2002b) with slight modi /uniFB01 cation. Brie /uniFB02 y, 2.5 mol/L of MgCl2 was added to the samples to obtain a /uniFB01 nal concentration of 25 mmol/L and the samples were passed through an electronegative membrane /uniFB01 lter (45 mm diameter, 0.45 m m pore size; Merck Millipore). After the binding of viruses and magnesium to the /uniFB01 lter, an acid rinse was performed by passing 200 mL of 0.5 mmol/L H2SO4 (pH 3.0) through the /uniFB01 lter, which dissolves and removes the bound magnesium from the /uniFB01 lter. Finally, the captured viruses were eluted with 10 mL of 1.0 mmol/L NaOH (pH 10.8). The eluate was recovered in a tube containing 50 m L of 100 mmol/L H2SO4 (pH 1.0) and 100 m L of 100 /C2 Tris-EDTA buffer (pH 8.0) for neutralization. The eluates were further concentrated using a Centriprep YM-50 /uniFB01 lter unit (Merck Millipore) to obtain a /uniFB01 nal volume of approximately 700 m L, according to the manufacturer's protocol.

Samples after CS and RSF were concentrated based on the method described by Hata et al. (2015a). Brie /uniFB02 y, the samples were suction /uniFB01 ltered through a sterilized tube where MgCl2 was continuously added in-line to obtain a /uniFB01 nal concentration of approximately 25 mmol/L. The samples were then passed through a cartridge type electronegative membrane /uniFB01 lter (Opticap XL2 disposable capsule /uniFB01 lters, 0.1 m 2 /uniFB01 ltration area, 0.5 m m pore size; Merck Millipore). Magnesium ions were subsequently removed by passing 2 L of 0.5 mmol/L H2SO4 (pH 3.0) through the /uniFB01 lter, and viruses were eluted with 200 mL of 1.0 mmol/L NaOH (pH 10.8). The eluate was recovered in a plastic bag containing 1 mL of 100 mmol/ L H2SO4 (pH 1.0) and 2 mL of 100 /C2 Tris-EDTA buffer (pH 8.0) for neutralization. The eluates were further concentrated using a Centricon plus-70 /uniFB01 lter unit (Merck Millipore) to obtain a /uniFB01 nal volume of approximately 1000 m L, according to the manufacturer's protocol. The concentrated samples were stored at /C0 20 /C14 C until further analysis.

## 2.4. Nucleic acid extraction and reverse transcription

Viral RNA and DNA in the concentrated samples were extracted using the QIAamp Viral RNA Mini Kit (Qiagen) and the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen), respectively, according to the manufacturer's protocol. The extracted viral RNA was subjected to reverse transcription (RT) using a High Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems). Brie /uniFB02 y, 20 m L of RT reaction mixture was prepared by mixing 10 m L of extracted RNA, 2 m L of 10 /C2 reverse transcription buffer, 0.8 m L of 25 /C2 dNTPs, 2 m L of 10 /C2 random primers, 1 m L of MultiScribe ™ reverse transcriptase (50 units/ m L), 1 m L of RNase inhibitor and 3.2 m L of UltraPure ™ DNase/RNase-Free Distilled Water (Invitrogen). ## 2.8. Calculation of the removal ef /uniFB01 ciency and statistical analysis

The removal ef /uniFB01 ciency of viruses, indicator bacteria, and turbidity by each water treatment process was calculated using log reduction values only when their concentration was quanti /uniFB01 ed both before and after the treatment process. For viruses, the removal ef /uniFB01 ciency was calculated only for the samples in which either the MNV internal control was quanti /uniFB01 ed with suf /uniFB01 ciently high ef /uniFB01 ciency ( &gt; 10%), or improvement of target virus concentrations was observed after puri /uniFB01 cation, in order to not overestimate or underestimate the removal ef /uniFB01 ciency. The highest concentration data obtained with or without the inhibitor reduction treatment was used to calculate the removal ef /uniFB01 ciency because the higher value was regarded as nearer to the true concentration. Statistical differences between removal rates of interest were evaluated by performing Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) tests using Microsoft Excel software. Whenever the log removal for a given treatment process could not be determined (virus was not detected before or after the treatment process), this statistical comparison could not be performed.

## 3.6. Removal ef /uniFB01 ciency of viruses, indicator bacteria, and turbidity

The removal ef /uniFB01 ciency of viruses (PMMoV and JC PyV), indicator bacteria ( E. coli and total coliforms), and turbidity are shown in Fig. 4 in the form of average log10 reduction values calculated using the paired data obtained from the water samples collected on the same day before and after each water treatment process. In this study, only PMMoV and JC PyV were found to have suf /uniFB01 cient positive results to be evaluated as viral fate indicators. For PMMoV, the highest concentration detected for a water sample with or without the puri /uniFB01 cation process was used to calculate the removal ef /uniFB01 -ciency. Virus quantities that were suspected to be underestimated (MNV detection &lt; 10%) and were not improved by the puri /uniFB01 cation process were omitted from this analysis. Considering the possibility that the removal ef /uniFB01 ciency of the water treatment processes could change according to seasonal variation in raw water quality, the removal ef /uniFB01 ciency of each target is shown separately for the wet season and the dry season.

During this study, removal ef /uniFB01 ciencies appeared to vary

between PMMoV and JC PyV for a given treatment process during the wet season, but not for the dry season. During the wet season, CS removed signi /uniFB01 cantly less PMMoV than JC PyV (0.40 ± 0.17 vs 1.91 ± 0.69 log10, p &lt; 0.01), while for RSF the reverse was true, with PMMoV removed signi /uniFB01 cantly more than JC PyV (1.26 ± 0.25 vs 0.49 ± 0.57 log10, p &lt; 0.01). This difference between the removal ef /uniFB01 ciencies may have been caused by a dissimilarity between the physico-chemical characteristics and/or shape of the different virus particles. For instance, the isoelectric point of a virus has been found to be an important parameter affecting their removal by CS (Abbaszadegan et al., 2008). The isoelectric point of PMMoV ranges from 3.38 to 3.80 and is known to be lower than that of many enteric viruses, including norovirus (5.5 e 6.0), rotavirus (8.0), and several enteroviruses (4 e 8.3) (Michen and Graule, 2010; Wetter et al., 1984). Furthermore, PMMoV is a rod-shaped virus with a width of 18 nm and the length of 312 nm, whereas JC PyV has an icosahedral capsid with a diameter of 45 nm, which could lead to differing coagulation ef /uniFB01 ciencies. In contrast, a difference in removal ef /uniFB01 ciency between these viruses was not observed during the dry season, where both PMMoV (1.61 log10 and 0.78 log10 for CS and RSF, respectively) and JC PyV (1.70 log10, and 0.59 log10, for CS and RSF, respectively) were removed predominantly by CS, although the signi /uniFB01 cance of this /uniFB01 nding is unclear due to the low number of removal ef /uniFB01 ciencies that were able to be calculated (n ¼ 1). Nevertheless, it is likely that the removal ef /uniFB01 ciencies of different viruses are dependent not only on the properties of the viruses themselves but also on the quality of the raw water as well. Albinana-Gimenez et al. (2009) reported on the removal ef /uniFB01 ciency of viruses at an actual plant scale by evaluating JC PyV, as in the case of the current study. According to their results, the removal ef /uniFB01 -ciency of JC PyV was 0.87 log10 after CS with a total reduction of 4.56 log10 observed after both CS and RSF, differing from the results obtained in the current study both in terms of overall removal ef/uniFB01 ciency and removal tendencies between processes. This gap between studies suggests that different coagulants, raw water quality, and modes of operation may greatly impact virus removal tendencies and ef /uniFB01 ciencies.

Overall PMMoV removal appeared to vary between wet and dry seasons, with overall less virus removed during the wet season than the dry season, whereas for JC PyV, overall virus removal ef/uniFB01 ciency appeared to be similar between seasons. Once again, due to the low number of calculable reductions during the dry season (n ¼ 1), the signi /uniFB01 cance of this comparison was not able to be calculated. The differences observed between the removal of these two viruses demonstrates the importance of observing virus removal in actual full-scale DWTPs, whose dynamic characteristics such as /uniFB02 uctuating seasonal water quality (e.g. turbidity) and DWTP operating parameters can impact the removal of different viruses. Furthermore, the relationship between the removal ef /uniFB01 ciency of these two viruses and that of viruses causing a signi /uniFB01 cant health risk (e.g. noroviruses) remains uncertain at the present stage. While it is desirable to determine the removal of actual pathogenic viruses in this setting, it is not possible to run spiked tests of pathogenic organisms at a full-plant scale. In this case, the best option for future studies is to compare the relative removal of these indicator viruses (PMMoV and JC PyV) and that of the pathogenic viruses of concern in pilot-scale or bench-scale experiments in order to con /uniFB01 rm whether removal ef /uniFB01 ciencies are comparable, thus con/uniFB01 rming the validity of targeting PMMoV and JC PyV for risk management.

The removal ef /uniFB01 ciency of indicator organisms was found to be slightly different between seasons. E. coli removal for CS was signi /uniFB01 cantly lower during the wet season than the dry season (1.26 ± 0.38 log10 and 1.66 ± 0.24 log10, respectively, p ¼ 0.04), while the reverse was observed for RSF removal (0.59 ± 0.50 log10

vs 0.06 ± 0.36 log10 for wet and dry season, respectively, p ¼ 0.049). The trends of removal ef /uniFB01 ciency for E. coli were similar to that observed for virus removal with the exception of PMMoV in the wet season, with PMMoV being removed signi /uniFB01 cantly less during CS and signi /uniFB01 cantly more during RSF (p &lt; 0.01 for both CS and RSF). Interestingly, the removal ef /uniFB01 ciency of total coliforms in the wet season appeared to be more balanced between CS and RSF (1.36 ± 0.34 log10 and 1.24 ± 0.87 log10, respectively), while reductions in the dry season (1.73 ± 0.42 log10, and 0.36 ± 0.66 log10, for CS and RSF respectively) were similar to those observed for E. coli . Overall removal was mainly due to CS as opposed to RSF treatment during the wet season for E. coli, and during the dry season for both bacterial indicators. For both PMMoV and indicator bacteria, the contribution of CS to the total removal ef /uniFB01 ciency appeared to increase and that of RSF decreased from the wet season to the dry season. In a previous pilot-scale study, it was reported that bacteria were removed mainly by RSF, while viruses were removed mainly by CS (Gerba et al., 2003). This appeared to be the case for PMMoV during the dry season and JC PyV (both seasons); however, the results for PMMoV during the wet season, E. coli (both seasons), and total coliforms during the dry season, appeared to be the opposite of those reported in the previous study. This gap suggests that a difference in the scale of the study (pilot vs fullplant scale) and/or seasonal trends in raw water quality may lead to differing removal tendencies and ef /uniFB01 ciencies. For example, in studies by Abbaszadegan et al. (2007, 2008), the removal ef /uniFB01 ciency of viruses at a pilot-scale was found to be greater than at a benchscale.

While differences in removal tendencies were observed for viruses and indicator bacteria, the removal tendencies of turbidity between the two seasons remained relatively the same with a smaller deviation observed than for the detected microorganisms. During the wet season the removal ef /uniFB01 ciency of turbidity was 1.22 ± 0.21 log10 for CS and 0.62 ± 0.28 log10 for RSF, with similar reductions observed during the dry season (1.02 ± 0.11 log10 and 0.48 ± 0.17 log10 for CS and RSF, respectively). The /uniFB02 uctuations in raw water turbidity were much higher in the wet season than in the dry season (Table 2 and Fig. 2); taken together with the low turbidity observed after treatment it appears that the dose rate of the coagulant (aluminum sulfate) was optimized by jar test (or some other means) for turbidity removal. In cases where turbidity was particularly high, increased concentrations of coagulant used by the DWTP may have led to increased removal of particles by sweep /uniFB02 occulation. If this was the case, it is possible that the decreased removal of PMMoV during CS processes may be due to differing /uniFB02 occulation behaviors of the virus in the presence of excess coagulant. Regardless of the potential mechanism, it is possible that this seasonal change in the removal of PMMoV is related to high turbidity in the raw water and suggests that such conditions may decrease the overall removal ef /uniFB01 ciency of some viruses in CS. In general, these results demonstrate that optimization for turbidity removal at a given operation does not necessarily lead to optimization of virus removal.

3.7. Possibility of a PMMoV/JC PyV-targeting risk management system

Based on the results of this study, PMMoV and JC PyV show potential as target viruses for managing the risk of viral infections from tap water using QMRA. In order to perform a risk assessment for a DWTP, the concentration of viruses in the raw water, which is easier to measure than that in the /uniFB01 nished water, needs to be determined along with the virus removal ef /uniFB01 ciencies for each water treatment process in order to estimate the virus concentration in the /uniFB01 nal water. In addition, in order to take uncertainty into

consideration and perform interval estimation, the removal ef /uniFB01 -ciencies should be evaluated as probability distributions; however, the removal ef /uniFB01 ciency of viruses at an actual-plant scale have rarely been evaluated due to the technical dif /uniFB01 culty associated with quantifying low concentrations of viruses in water (Gibson, 2014). In the Netherlands, where QMRA is practiced on a regular basis for evaluating drinking water safety, removal ef /uniFB01 ciency has been previously evaluated in the form of a triangular distribution using data obtained from the literature (Smeets et al., 2006). Triangular distributions are made of the lowest, the highest, and the mean elimination capacity value, with the mean value weighted by the research scale (bench-scale, pilot-scale, or actual plant-scale). One disadvantage of using triangular distributions are that they lack in precision and accuracy because the data from bench-scale and pilot-scale studies are often used due to the lack of data from fullscale DWTPs. Furthermore, triangular distributions do not take into consideration differences in operational conditions because they typically use the average of more than two literature data that may be derived from processes under different circumstance, thereby disregarding important causative factors such as raw water quality. PMMoV or JC PyV could be used in this case to provide more accurate virus removal data and with repeated sampling could also be used to generate probability distributions for better incorporation into risk models. These target viruses were detected frequently in raw water samples and, in general, did not appear to vary by season as shown by their low standard deviation over the sampling period ( ± 0.35 for PMMoV and ± 0.42 for JC PyV), therefore they could be monitored under a variety of different operational conditions to determine site speci /uniFB01 c removal ef /uniFB01 ciencies for more accurate QMRA.

## 4. Conclusions

- /C15 The removal ef /uniFB01 ciency of indigenous viruses at an actual DWTP in Bangkok, Thailand was successfully determined by concentrating large volume water samples and applying a puri /uniFB01 cation process that utilized DAX-8 resin to improve virus detection.
- /C15 PMMoV and JC PyV were found to be potential targets for determining removal ef /uniFB01 ciency due to their high prevalence in water environments with little to no seasonal change in their concentrations during this study.
- /C15 The concentrations of PMMoV and JC PyV in DWTP raw water samples was high enough to remain above the detection limit even after typical physico-chemical water treatment processes (coagulation/sedimentation, rapid sand /uniFB01 ltration), allowing for determination of step-wise virus removal.
- /C15 Virus removal ef /uniFB01 ciency was found to vary based on raw water quality and the target virus type, therefore further work is still needed to assess whether the relative removal of PMMoV and/or JC PyV is comparable to that of pathogenic enteric viruses.
- /C15 PMMoV and JC PyV represent promising options for monitoring the fate of viruses in actual DWTPs when the low concentration of enteric viruses prevents their direct observation and could be useful for modeling the risk of viral infection in treated tap water.
