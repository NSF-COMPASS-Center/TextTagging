Keywords: Rotavirus QMRA Drinking water Diarrhoea Water quality

Rotavirus is one of the main waterborne causes of diarrhoea. Rotavirus type A is responsible for diarrhoea in infants and causes thousands of deaths annually around the world, especially in developing countries. Rotavirus is very small and is highly resistant to common disinfectants, so the World Health Organization (WHO) has chosen this virus as a reference pathogen in drinking water and has recommended a its 6-log removal by the conventional water treatment process to supply safe drinking water. We measured some physical and chemical characteristics of raw water at a water treatment plant in Tehran, Iran, including temperature, pH, total organic compound (TOC) concentration and initial turbidity, to determine their effect on rotavirus removal efficiency in various processes. We then measured rotavirus removal efficiency in clarified water and filtrate to determine their removal efficiency. Finally, we applied the WHO guideline and an empirical quantitative microbial risk assessment (QMRA) model to estimate probable health risk based on a residual number of rotavirus in finished water. We found that TOC concentration and water temperature are both effective on residual rotavirus in clarified water but they have no significant effect on the efficacy of filtration in rotavirus removal. Maximum rotavirus removal efficiency by clarification and filtration was 97.2% and 4.5% in April and January respectively. TOC and water temperature were shown to have a significant effect on clarification virus efficiency, so filtration performance was independent of these variables. The results showed that induced risk by residual rotavirus is in an acceptable range (99.99945% removal annually), and also showed that the critical process in virus removal is clarification that can be affected by raw water quality.

Globally, most water treatment plant sites use conventional processes including coagulation, flocculation, clarification, sand filtration and disinfection. As WHO mentioned, the efficiency of these processes in the removal of various pathogens is different (Edition, 2011). The probable variations in the operation and maintenance of the conventional treatment process has a minimal effect on bacteria removal efficiency, so there is a high degree of confidence in the removal of bacteria by conventional treatment processes (Shannon et al., 2010). Moreover, bacteria are sensitive to chlorine and can be efficiently removed by disinfection (Black et al., 2009). Protozoa are highly resistant to chlorine but, because of their large size, can be efficiently removed by physical separation processes. It is, however, a different story for viral pathogens. Waterborne viruses are moderately resistant to chlorine, are very small and generally have a low infectious dose, so WHO has recommended 6-log removal for enteric viruses to prevent a diarrhoea outbreak. Several enteric viruses are mentioned in drinking water quality guidelines as indicators, but the reference virus is Rotavirus (World Health Organization, 2004).

Rotavirus is a double-stranded RNA genus of viruses in the Reoviridae family. Rotavirus has nine species referred to as A, B, C, D, E, F, G, H and I. Type A is the most common cause of infectious diarrhoea among infants and young children worldwide (Murray et al., 2015). They are also pathogens of animals and livestock. Each gram of faeces from an infected person contains more than 10 trillion pfu/gr of viruses, but less than 100 of them can transmit infection and make someone else sick (Sadiq et al., 2018). These viruses are stable in the environment and, even after a month, have still been found in water samples such as rivers (Miura et al., 2019). They have a great effect on health, and are relatively resistant to chlorine, so WHO has indicated rotaviruses as reference pathogens in drinking water (World Health Organization, 2004).

Several sanitary actions such as health education, sanitary disposal and the treatment of wastewater have reduced the rotavirus diarrhoea incidence rate (Sano et al., 2016), but the most critical action was water treatment and safe drinking water supply. However, rotavirus 'A' infection is endemic worldwide, and causes millions of cases of diarrhoea in developing countries every year, which resulted in almost 2 million annual hospitalisations around the world. In 2013, an estimated 215,000 deaths of children younger than five were attributable to rotavirus (Simpson et al., 2007; Tate et al., 2016). Rotavirus is one of the greatest causes of severe diarrhoea among infants and children, and causes 5% of all deaths in children younger than five and 37% of deaths attributable to diarrhoea (Tate et al., 2012).

Because of the health-related significance of rotaviruses, several studies were done to monitor rotaviruses in raw or finished water to protect public health (Miura et al., 2019). Because of the negligible effect of routine disinfectants on the de-activation of rotaviruses, the major process for their removal is solid-liquid separation including clarification and sand filtration. However, their efficiency can be affected by several operational and maintenance modes such as coagulation condition, type of filter media and filtration flow rate, so the efficiency of the viruses' removal, especially rotavirus in the pre-filtration process (coagulation, flocculation, sedimentation), can be from 0.1 to 3.4 log and from 0 to 3.5 log in the filtration process (Pooi and Ng, 2018). Subsequently, these processes can be affected by raw water characteristics, so the number of rotaviruses in finished water can differ under different conditions, creating various levels of infection risk. Several coun- tries have rotavirus vaccination programmes. As WHO reported in Rotavirus Immunization Coverage Estimates , the global coverage of rotavirus C vaccination among one-year-old children was just 25% in 2016 (Tate et al., 2016). This programme can prevent the incidence of rotavirus infection due to drinking water or food, but many children around the world are not covered by a vaccination programme. Moreover, this programme is targeted at rotavirus 'C', which is a cause of adult diarrhoea, and there is no immunisation programme against rotavirus 'A' so, if the number of rotaviruses increases in drinking water for any reason, this can lead to a viral diarrhoea outbreak, especially in neonatal children and infants. As a result, the monitoring of rotaviruses in drinking water is necessary. The aim of this study is to investigate the influence of raw water characteristics including temperature, pH, the initial number of rotaviruses in treated water and change in induced microbial health risk by quantitative microbial risk assessment (QMRA).

## 2. Material and methods

## 2.1. Water treatment plant description

A drinking water treatment plants (DWTP) in Tehran (the capital of Iran) that treating surface water was engaged for this study. The treatment process entailed coagulation in a rapid mixing unit using FeCl3 as coagulant, flocculation in pulsation, clarification, rapid sand filtration and finally disinfection by Cl2 . The Fig. 1 is schematic flow diagram of existing processes in this DWTP. The key parameter for operation and maintenance was turbidity, so the DWTP tried to reduce the clarified water's turbidity below 1 NTU and the filtrate's turbidity below 0.1 NTU. The main additive in raw water was coagulant (FeCl3) and the finished water did not need pH adjustment. We monitored this DWTP for a year from August 2017 until July 2018. Measurements were taken at three points in the DWTP including raw water, clarified water (before filtration) and filtrated water (before disinfection). The samples were taken on the 5 th , 15 th and 25 th days of every month (three days in every month) and the measurements were repeated three times. Three parameters including pH, total organic compound (TOC) concentration and temperature were chosen for measurement as they can affect coagulation and clarification efficiency., Turbidity, particle count and rotavirus copy number were also chosen and measured for investigation of the relationship between the microbiological and physical quality of treated water. In each sampling, two separate samples were captured simultaneously including a one-litre sample for chemical characteristics and a 10-litre sample for the extraction of rotavirus.

## 2.2. Chemical and physical quality of water samples

The measured parameters included total organic compound (TOC) concentration, pH, turbidity, particle count and temperature. The pH and temperature of the samples were measured in-situ. Turbidity was measured by a turbidimeter (TU5 series, HATCH, USA) and reported as NTU units, then the particles were counted by a liquid particle counter (CHEMTRACPC3400, USA). After that, the samples were filtered through a 0.8 𝛍 m fibreglass filter and introduced to the TOC meter (Analytik-Jena, Germany).

## 2.4. RNA extraction

The real-time PCR technique was used to determine the rotavirus number in the samples. Briefly, the filters were first weighed, then suspended in 10% w/v phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Viral RNA was extracted from 200 ml of 10% suspension by the guanidinium isothiocyanate method. Size-fractionated silica was then used for sample purification. In every RNA extraction process, a known rotavirus-positive sample (positive control) and an equivalent volume of PBS (negative control) were included.

## 2.5. cDNA synthesis

RT reactions were carried out in a final volume of 20 𝛍 l using a reverse transcription kit (Gen Biotek, Korea) on a GeneAmp 9700 thermocycler from Applied Biosystems. The reactions contained a 10 𝛍 l RT buffer, a 2 𝛍 l combination of deoxynucleoside triphosphate (dNTP), a 2 𝛍 l random hexamer, 0.5 𝛍 l reverse transcriptase, and 5 𝛍 l of extracted nucleic acids. To make the reverse transcription of the double-stranded RNA of rotaviruses easy, reaction components were first denatured by heating at 95 ◦ C for 5 min and then immediately put on ice for 5 min, and master mix was then added to the reaction. Thermal cycling parameters for the RT reactions were as follows: 10 min at 25 ◦ C, 40 min at 42 ◦ C, and 5 min at 95 ◦ C. Completed RT reaction samples were stored at -20 ◦ C for further analysis.

## 2.7. QMRA protocol

In this study, we tried to determine the burden of infection attributable to existing rotavirus in finished water and, for comparison, with a reference level risk set by WHO. QMRA steps include exposure assessment, dose-effect determination and risk characterisation. Exposure assessment includes measurement of the number of pathogens in drinking water that can be ingested. The dose-effect relationship can be determined by fitting a model for observed adverse health effects against the number of ingested pathogens and, finally in risk characterisation, the infection possibility can be defined as a 'product of exposure' to an individual pathogen. WHO set 1 × 10 -6 as the annual reference level risk for waterborne pathogens. In this study, we will compare the determined risk with this value. To conduct QMRA, we used two methods. The first method was the WHO procedure for low-dose pathogens in drinking water (World Health Organization (2016)).

The results showed that overall DWTP efficiency for virus removal was 99.99943% (5.5 log), which matches the WHO guideline. According to this result, the disease burden by application of the QMRA method is summarised in Table 6.

It is noticeable that the water drinking rate is assumed to be 1 L per person per day. As mentioned above, the particle count showed correlation with the rotavirus count in clarified water. On the other hand, there was no correlation between them in filtrate. Several studies have been done on the relationship between particle count and the number of pathogens in treated water. LeChevallier et al, found a direct relationship between giardia and cryptosporidium with particle count in filtrate water (LeChevallier and Norton, 1992). By contrast, in regard to the population of bacteria, McCoy et al. found no relationship between particle count and the colony count of water samples (McCoy and Olson, 1986), but Ender et al. found strong correlation between particle count and the number of E. coli (Ender et al., 2017). We can thus see very different relations between particle count and various kinds of pathogens. In our results, the mean of particle removal by filtration was about 67%, so the mean of virus removal from total virus in clarified water was 93%. This shows that most viruses in clarified water are free and not attached to colloids, so greater efficiency in virus removal is dependent on efficient flocculation. Abudalo, R. et al. and S. et al. confirmed this concept. They mentioned that the coagulation-sedimentation process is critical in rotavirus removal. They reported that higher collision efficiency in flocculation can increase virus number in clarified water (Abudalo et al., 2005; Lee et al., 2017).

Another important factor in rotavirus removal is pH. Most of the most frequent viruses in raw or treated water (enterovirus, adenovirus, rotavirus and hepatitis A) are non-enveloped viruses. The Iso electric point (IEP) of rotavirus A is at pH = 8. Its IEP is significantly higher than the IEP of the other most frequent non-enveloped viruses such as adenovirus (IEP = 2.6), enterovirus (IEP = 4.5-6.1) and hepatitis A (IEP = 2.8) in raw or treated water (Michen and Graule, 2010). As shown in the results, the raw water pH did not exceed 8 at any time of year and the maximum pH was 8. After coagulation by FeCl3, pH fell. This effect helps viruses to be more stable and avoid flocculation, so pH falling to about 6 can enhance the neutralisation of enterovirus surface charge and improve its removal efficiency.

Several studies investigate the number of rotaviruses in drinking water and the risk attributable to the burden of the disease by QMRA. Determination of the burden of rotavirus at two DWTPs in Uganda shows a risk of 7.1E-03 and 7.88E-05. These values are higher than the results of this study and also higher than the reference level risk (Howard et al., 2006). Moreover, the daily infection risk of rotavirus attributable to treated water from the Buffalo River in Cape Town was reported at 6.54E-01, which is more than in our study (Chigor et al., 2014). Furthermore, the number of rotaviruses in the drinking water of Maysor city (India) was monitored for a 24-month period for QMRA. The results showed that the burdens of rotavirus infection attributable to two DWTPs in this city were 4.75E-07 and 2.54E-04(23). The WHO recommended 6-log rotavirus removal in its drinking water quality guideline. It stated that appropriate rotavirus removal by a conventional DWTP can be between 5.5 and 7 log. As mentioned above, the efficiency of this DWTP was 99.99935% (5.5 log). This is an acceptable efficiency. Although TOC showed high variation in different months, the main factor causing such a high removal efficiency was that the DWTP tried to keep clarified water turbidity around 1 NTU. When TOC is high, effective flocculation needs the application of extra coagulant (enhanced coagulation). This can reduce water pH and the finished water might need pH adjustment, but it can aggregate free colloids such as free virus and bacteria and improve pathogen removal efficiency. Another problem of TOC remaining in finished water is disinfectant consumption (Mao et al., 2018). Residual disinfectant in drinking water is necessary to increase the removal efficiency of enteric viruses to a level with an acceptable health risk (Emelko et al., 2019). Since many drinking water disinfectants have an oxidative feature, remaining TOC can reduce disinfectant concentration in finished water and protect any remaining viral and bacterial pathogens against disinfectant (Collivignarelli et al., 2017).

## 5. Conclusion

Convectional drinking water treatment processes can remove rotavirus efficiently, satisfy the WHO guideline value for rotavirus in drinking water and decrease infection risk below the risk level recommended by WHO. Therefore, in societies with conventional water treatment plant sites with appropriate maintenance, the rotavirus cannot induce major health concerns from drinking water. As indicated in this study, the removal efficiency is between 97.3 and 99.99965% and most of the rotavirus population can be removed by clarification that is closely dependent on the quality of coagulation and flocculation. Increasing TOC and decreasing water temperature can decrease clarification efficiency for solid-liquid separation whose indicator is particle count in clarified water. The efficiency of rapid sand filtration is more sensitive to water temperature, so maintaining the removal efficiency of pre-filtration processes at a constant level by change in coagulant dosage and mixing power to keep the turbidity of clarified water below 0.1 NTU, can recover tolerance in the efficiency of filtration.
