ABSTRACT: Understanding the behavior of coronaviruses during wastewater treatment processes is imperative, but little is known about the fate of coronaviruses during the coagulation of municipal wastewater by ferric chloride. This study investigated the fate of two surrogate coronaviruses, i.e., feline infectious peritonitis virus (FIPV) and human coronavirus 229E (HCoV-229E), by measuring their infectivity in filtered and unfiltered raw wastewater subject to ferric coagulation. The dynamics of relevant wastewater parameters, including pH, COD (chemical oxygen demand), phosphate, sulfide, and iron species, were also profiled in parallel. The entrapment of coronaviruses during the process of sweep coagulation, in combination with the electrostatic force between viruses and iron precipitates such as ferric phosphate and ferric oxyhydroxides, is believed to be responsible for the enhanced removal of viruses from the liquid phase of wastewater during ferric coagulation. These findings provide insight into the environmental fate of coronaviruses in wastewater treatment and raise awareness about the importance of safe disposal of wastewater sludge.

## 1. INTRODUCTION

Coronaviridae (coronavirus) is a family of single-stranded RNA viruses that are responsible for several severe respiratory infections, including severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus (SARS-CoV), Middle Eastern respiratory syndrome coronavirus (MERS-CoV), and severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2). SARS-CoV-2 is responsible for the Coronavirus Disease-19 (COVID-19) pandemic and has spread globally over the past three years through human-to-human transmission via virus-laden aerosols. This highly contagious virus also found its way into the urban wastewater systems through the shedding of feces or other contaminated bodily fluids. 1,2 Thus, wastewater can serve as a potential vector for the spread of the virus. It has been suggested that the sewer stacks in high-rise buildings may act as a transmission channel for the spread of two coronavirus-caused diseases, i.e., COVID-19 and SARS. 3 -7 While it is generally believed that the coronavirus may not be able to remain infectious by the time it reaches wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs), the possibility of fecal -oral transmission may become a concern because the virus can survive in feces and wastewater for a certain period of time. Therefore, it is important to consider the potential for coronavirus to spread through wastewater and take appropriate wastewater treatment measures

In order to study the behavior of highly pathogenic coronaviruses in wastewater, researchers commonly use surrogate coronaviruses as representatives of these dangerous human pathogens, which are difficult to handle and require a higher level of safety facilities. Feline infectious peritonitis virus (FIPV) as an enteric virus and human coronavirus 229E (HCoV-229E) as a respiratory virus in the Coronaviridae family are widely accepted as surrogates for coronaviruses (enveloped, single-stranded RNA viruses whose diameter ranges from 60 to 220 nm) because of their similarity in size, composition, and morphology. 12,13 Additionally, other coronaviruses including the transmissible gastroenteritis virus (TGEV), murine hepatitis virus (MHV), and the enveloped bacteriophage Phi6 have also

Chemical dosing is broadly utilized for corrosion mitigation in sewer networks, nutrient removal in biological reactors, and suspended solid (SS) removal by coagulation during wastewater treatment. 19 -21 Ferric chloride is one of the most frequently used chemicals in municipal wastewater systems as an efficient strategy for controlling H2S emission as well as removing phosphorus and organic matter in WWTPs. 22,23 Kulandaivelu et al. 24 also found the additional benefit of dosing FeCl 3 to the insewer removal of organic micropollutants before entering into the WWTPs, which was attributed to the rapid adsorption on iron precipitates. More importantly, the coagulating nature of iron salt and adsorbing nature of iron precipitates 25,26 support the hypothesis that ferric dosing will enhance virus removal during the coagulation of wastewater. No study has investigated the effect of ferric dosing on coronavirus removal in wastewater to date. Hence, the current understanding of coronavirus reduction subjected to ferric coagulation is very limited.

This study aims to gain a deeper understanding of the fate of coronaviruses during wastewater transportation and treatment processes by focusing on the effects of ferric dosing. Specifically, the study aims to (i) determine the natural decay of surrogate coronaviruses in filtered wastewater through survivability tests using spiked infectious viruses; (ii) quantify the physical removal of viruses due to attachment to iron precipitates and wastewater solids after ferric coagulation; and (iii) investigate the potential mechanisms of virus adsorption to various matrixes in wastewater by profiling dynamics of relevant wastewater parameters and characterizing precipitated solids. The results obtained from this study are expected to provide useful data and information for the management of wastewater treatment and the prevention of coronavirus spread.

## 2. MATERIALS AND METHODS

## 2.1. Cultivation and Titration of Surrogate Coronavi-

ruses. Chemicals and media ingredients used in the experiments were purchased from Life Technologies, Australia. Original viruses and cell lines were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) (In Vitro Technologies, Australia). Detailed procedures for cell culture and virus preparation have been reported by Shi et al. 18 Briefly, the feline infectious peritonitis virus (FIPV) (ATCC VR-990) was propagated and titrated in the Crandell Reese feline kidney (CRFK) cell line (ATCC CCL-94), while human coronavirus 229E (HCoV) (ATCC VR-740) was propagated and titrated in the fetal human lung fibroblast MRC-5 cell line (ATCC CCL171).

The virus was cultured by inoculating a viral seed after its host cell line was grown to 80% confluency in Dulbecco's modified eagle medium (DMEM) with 10% serum, and the medium had been changed into DMEM with 2% serum in the flask. When the cytopathic effect (CPE) of the hosting cells was obvious under a Leica DMi1 inverted microscope (Leica, Germany) after incubation for around 48 -66 h, the resulting suspension medium with the majority of viruses was centrifuged (1000 g , 5 min) and filtered through 0.45 μ m poly(ether sulfone) (PES) membrane filters (Sarstedt, Germany) to remove cell debris. Then, the virus was concentrated by ultracentrifugation at 85,000 g for 4 h at 4 ° C using a Sorvall LYNX 6000 superspeed centrifuge (Thermo Scientific, Australia). The collected virus pellet was resuspended in cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) solution after discarding the supernatant. The virus stock was titrated and stored in 1.5 mL sterile tubes at -80 ° Cuntil further use.

The viral concentration of virus stock and wastewater samples was indicated by the median tissue culture infectious dose (TCID50) in 96-well plates. The TCID50 technique determines the dilution at which 50% of the wells (with eight replicates) show the CPE after adding a serial dilution of the sample with viruses to host cells. The plates with 10 μ L inoculation of the sample in each well were regularly monitored under a microscope within a week, and well numbers showing CPE were recorded. The dilution at which 50% of the wells showed CPE was calculated and multiplied by 100 to reveal virus titer per mL since the inoculation volume was 10 μ L. Finally, the inverse log of the specific dilution was taken as the virus titer and expressed as TCID50/mL. 18

2.2. Batch Tests of Wastewater Coagulation with Ferric Chloride. Rawwastewater was collected from the inlet of a municipal wastewater treatment plant in Wollongong, Australia and subjected to characterization according to the description in section 2.4 (Table S1). The filtered wastewater was prepared by filtering raw wastewater through filter paper with 0.45 μ m pore size.

Toprofile the dynamics of wastewater parameters after adding ferric chloride (FeCl3 · 6H2O), batch coagulation tests in triplicate were conducted in 1 L bottles at room temperature (20 ± 2 ° C). An amount of 1 L filtered or unfiltered wastewater in each bottle was added with ferric chloride to the initial concentration of 10 mg Fe/L, and the bottles containing wastewater were shaken rigorously for 1 min. At time points of 5 min, 30 min, 1 h, 2 h, 4 h, and 6 h following the addition, aliquot portions of wastewater samples were withdrawn from the bottle and subjected to the determination of parameters, including pH, COD, sulfide, phosphate, total iron, soluble iron, and ferrous iron.

After 6 h profile experiments, the precipitates in both filtered and unfiltered wastewater were collected by filtration through 0.45 μ m filter paper and desiccation. In addition, the original suspended solids in raw wastewater were also collected in the same way. Fourier transform infrared spectrophotometer (FTIR) analysis of the wastewater particles was conducted on an IRAffinity-1 FTIR spectroscope (Shimadzu). Samples were directly mounted on the diamond accessory for scanning, and the background scan was deducted for each measurement. An average of 20 scans were obtained for each sample, followed by spectral processing with LabSolutions IR software for smoothing and peak picking. The spectral data were exported to .txt files and plotted in Origin 8 software. The differential peaks among the treatments were identified and assigned to relevant functional groups as listed in Table S3. 24,25

2.3. Survivability Tests of Coronaviruses in Wastewater with Ferric Coagulation. The survivability experiments (in triplicate) of two surrogate coronaviruses were conducted in both filtered and unfiltered wastewater with or without the addition of ferric chloride to the concentration of 10 mg Fe/L in 50 mL conical tubes at room temperature (20 ± 2 ° C). To further identify the role of coagulation in virus partitioning and removal, two treatments were designed for experiments with ferric dosing: one ferric dosing prior to the virus and the other adding viral stock before the dosing of ferric chloride.

The prepared viral stock was spiked in and mixed with 30 mL of wastewater in each tube to the initial concentration of 10 5 TCID50/mL. Following the dosage of ferric chloride, it was thoroughly mixed with wastewater by shaking the conical tubes for 1 min to make sure FeCl3 could be fully dispersed for coagulation. 2.4. Analysis of Wastewater Parameters. Wastewater pH and dissolved oxygen (DO) were measured by an ST310 pH electrode (Ohaus, USA) and HI98196 multiparameter (Hanna Instruments, Australia), respectively. The total suspended solid (TSS) of the wastewater was determined by weighing the 0.45 μ m filter paper before and after wastewater filtration, drying at 105 ° C, and desiccation. Chemical oxygen demand (COD) was determined by a TNT plus COD test kit with a Hach DR3900 spectrometer (Hach, Australia) according to the US-EPA Standard Method 5220D. Sulfate (SO4 2 -) and sulfide (S 2 -) were analyzed by SulfaVer 4 sulfate reagent powder pillows and a MERK sulfide test kit (1.14779), respectively. Orthophosphate concentration (PO4 -P or PO4 3 -) was determined with a TNT plus 846 phosphorus test kit by HACH method 10214. Total iron and ferrous iron (Fe 2+ ) concentrations were measured by HACH methods 8112 and 8146, respectively. Dissolved iron was measured by the same method as total iron (HACH method 8112) but after the wastewater sample was filtered through a 0.2 μ m filter. The concentration of ferric iron (Fe 3+ ) can be determined as the difference between the results of the ferrous iron and the concentration of total soluble iron.

## 3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Visually, the ferric precipitates (FPs) formed in filtered wastewater after the addition of FeCl 3 were yellowish green in color (as shown in Figure S3), which suggests the presence of iron oxyhydroxide or possibly ferric (hydr)oxide in the mixture. On the other hand, in raw wastewater, the addition of FeCl 3 led to the formation of agglomerated flocs (i.e., coagulated solids, CSs) from the dispersed suspended solids (SSs). The larger, more easily separable CS particles can facilitate virus removal from the wastewater through flocculation and sedimentation.

3.1. Profiles of Surrogate Coronaviruses in Wastewater Coagulation. The surrogate coronaviruses survive the best in filtered wastewater without ferric coagulation, where they follow biphasic first-order decay kinetics (Figure 1 and Table S2). The decay rate constants of FIPV (1.38 for the first phase and 0.07 for the second phase) are greater than those of HCoV229E (0.73 for the first phase and 0.05 for the second phase) in filtered wastewater (Table S2). The data indicate that the HCoV-229E virus needs a longer time to achieve a two-log reduction, with a T 99 value of 79.0 h, compared to the FIPV virus which has a T 99 value of 56.2 h.

The decay kinetic analysis reveals that the infective coronaviruses exhibited similar rates of decrease after the initial 2 h of ferric coagulation, as demonstrated by the parallel curves observed in Figure 1. Furthermore, it was observed that a 2 h period is sufficient for solid particles or precipitates in wastewater to settle at the bottom of the conical tubes. As the viral titer was only detected in the liquid component of the wastewater, it can be inferred that the decay rates 2 h after the introduction of the viruses into the raw wastewater containing solids should be similar to those observed in filtered wastewater without solids.

During the initial 2 h, the reduction of both surrogate coronaviruses in raw wastewater was observed to be significantly faster compared to that in filtered wastewater. This is likely attributed to the partitioning and adsorption of virus particles to the suspended solids present in the wastewater. A previous simulation study further indicated that this virus partitioning process in wastewater can take up to 2 h. 28 This supports the hypothesis that virus adsorption to solid particles is the cause of the rapid decrease in viral titer observed in this study. The difference in the decreasing slope (which serves as an indicator of decay rates) is most pronounced during the first 20 min. After this point, the viruses in ferric-dosed treatments exhibit a similar decreasing trend as those without the addition of FeCl 3 .

To delineate the effects of the order of adding ferric chloride and spiking coronaviruses in wastewater, two parallel tests were carried out. In filtered wastewater, this order did not have a significant impact on virus removal, resulting in approximately 1.3-log reductions for both surrogate viruses (Figure 1). However, in raw wastewater, the decrease of viruses with ferric dosing after virus addition was found to occur at a faster rate than that of ferric dosing prior to virus addition. Even the viral reduction in raw wastewater without coagulation was faster than that of ferric dosing prior to virus addition.

3.2. Effects of the Ferric Coagulation Process on Virus Removal. Normally, coagulation occurs within a few minutes after rapid mixing, as stated in a study by Kim et al. 25 In this study, the pH of the wastewater was found to be in the range 6 -

8 (Figure S1). This allows for the hydrolysis of ferric ions, resulting in the precipitation of ferric hydroxide. These hydrolytes can destabilize colloids in the wastewater by overcoming the repulsive forces (energy barrier) between colloidal particles, leading to agglomeration. Additionally, the decrease in the concentration of iron species (Figure S2) may be due to its incorporation with sulfide, phosphate, or organic matter, forming precipitates and resulting in further sedimentation, which is evidenced by the coincidental decrease in the concentrations of sulfide and phosphate (Figure S1). These ferric precipitates (FPs) can cover the negatively charged SSs (215 mg/L as in Table S1) in the wastewater and form compacted flocs (coagulated solids, CSs).

Furthermore, since the pH is in the range of 6 -8 and the concentration of ferric chloride is around 50 mg/L (10 mg of Fe/L), sweep coagulation should be the predominant process happening in the system according to the ferric chloride solubility -pH diagram (Figure S4). 29 These facilitated partitioning processes can trap viruses in CS and separate them from the wastewater supernatant. The enhanced removal of coronaviruses by ferric coagulation (Figure 1) is thus attributed to the entrapment during the formation of larger CSs in the initial 20 min after FeCl 3 addition.

Coagulation with ferric chloride significantly improved virus removal, to around a 2.6-log decrease for FIPV. This level of removal is about 0.9-log higher than the attachment of FIPV to wastewater SSs or to the FPs formed in filtered wastewater, which are at similar levels (Table 1). Additionally, FeCl3 in wastewater induces coagulation and flocculation by developing affinitive bonding among suspended solids. Viral particles would be entrained into the CS mass flocs (Figure S3) during coagulation and flocculation, which usually happens within 20 min. Once such bonding has been performed, the specific surface area of wastewater solids available for the attachment of viruses could be greatly reduced. Also, the positively charged FP was neutralized by the negatively charged SS during the coagulation. Therefore, the less positively charged PCShaslower adsorption capacity for coronaviruses. During the 2 h sedimentation following the 20 min of coagulation, an adsorption equilibrium of viral particles was reached. Concomitantly, most wastewater parameters except pH also reached a steady state by about 2 h. Overall, the synchronizing profiles of viruses and iron precipitation suggest the important role of viral adsorption onto the ferric precipitates.

However, it is still important to undertake better disinfection measures in order to minimize the potential of infection.

## 3.5. Comparison with Other Studies and Implications.

The virus mitigation by coagulation has been critically reviewed in detail by Heffron and Mayer, 26 which is impacted by electrostatic forces, the hydrophobic effect, steric hindrance, and hydrodynamics interactions in the water. Most previous coagulation experiments were conducted with alum-based coagulants like PACl, AlCl3, and aluminum electrodes in drinking water treatments, by focusing on the removal of nonenveloped enteric viruses. Ferric chloride is not only used as a coagulant but also widely applied in sewers for corrosion and odor control. Some previous studies investigated the effect of ferric coagulation on the virus removal, which are summarized in Table 2.

Kim et al. 25 reported the rapid 5-log removal in a few minutes and inactivation by oxidative damage on the enveloped lipid of surrogate virus ϕ 6 due to iron conventional coagulation and electrocoagulation in synthetic natural water. However, ϕ 6 may not be a perfect representative for enveloped coronavirus because it showed varied persistence in aqueous conditions from pathogenic human viruses. 44 The level of virus removal by coagulation also varied greatly among viral species (Table 2). The observed diverse range of virus removal rates might result from their different partitioning behavior and adsorption to solid fractions in an aquatic environment, 28 particularly between enveloped viruses (26% absorbed) and nonenveloped viruses

It is believed that coronaviruses, as an enveloped virus which was reported to be more susceptible to decay than nonenveloped viruses, 45 may not remain infectious in wastewater when arriving at wastewater treatment plants. Only a few studies have investigated the fate of infective coronaviruses during wastewater treatment processes. The handling of infectious human coronaviruses in experiments is a biosafety concern and requires a high safety level of lab facilities. Such difficulties may have led to the limited data available on the removal rates of infectious coronaviruses during wastewater treatment.

Studies on coronavirus removal are largely based on qPCR detection of nucleic acid fragments, which is different from the TCID50 enumeration of infectious coronaviruses. 46 Exceptionally, Greaves et al. 47 reported a 3-log 10 TCID50 reduction of infectious SARS-CoV-2 by more than 5 mg min/L of sodium hypochlorite (free chlorine) disinfection of influent wastewater. The reported virus inactivation was more efficient compared with the virus removal by 10 mg of Fe/L of ferric chloride, which only resulted in less than 3-log 10 TCID50 reduction after coagulation in this study.

It should be noted that the removal of coronaviruses by coagulation is different from their inactivation by disinfectants. The viruses removed from the water phase may remain infectious and be concentrated in the coagulated flocs. Thus, the resulting wastewater sludge may have a high coronavirus load, 48,49 which needs to be disinfected properly. Based on findings from this study, further in-depth investigation should be conducted to delineate the fate of coronaviruses in wastewater under dynamic conditions at a larger scale with diverse ranges of chemical dosages and wastewater characteristics.

This is the first study of the partitioning and removal of infectious coronaviruses in domestic wastewater with ferric chloride as the coagulant. The observations from our experiments help with delineating the fate of viruses in urban water systems and understanding the potential mechanism. However, the infectivity of virus-laden solids in wastewater was not quantified in this study due to the difficulty and known low efficiency of virus recovery from solid particles. 50,51 The particleattached viruses may remain infectious for a longer time than in

the water phase due to the protective effect from solid particles. 52 Further enumeration of infective viruses attached to solids might provide useful information on virus removal and inactivation, possibly resulting from the oxidative stress of ferric species. 25

Investigation of virus-laden solids will not only benefit the partitioning study of coronavirus in wastewater systems but also contribute knowledge to the treatment of sludge. Other than solid precipitates produced by dosing coagulant, biomass or biofilms in urban water systems like sewers or wastewater treatment facilities can also capture coronaviruses. 18,53,54 Clarifying the portion of the coronavirus attachment to various matrixes helps to track the virus fate and quantify their distribution in municipal wastewater systems.

## 4. CONCLUSIONS

This study investigated the decay and removal of infectious surrogate coronaviruses in filtered and unfiltered raw wastewater with or without ferric dosing at 10 mg Fe/L. The results lead to the following conclusions:

- Coronaviruses FIPV and HCoV-229E followed biphasic first-order decay kinetics in filtered wastewater. HCoV229E ( T 99 =79.0 h) survived better than FIPV ( T 99 =56.2 h) under the given experimental conditions (temperature 20 ± 2 ° C, pH 7.9, COD around 188 mg/L).
- Ferric dosing in filtered wastewater generated various ferric precipitates (with phosphorus, sulfide, and organic compounds) that significantly enhanced the removal of the two surrogate coronaviruses by over 1-log reduction, which occurred mostly within the initial 20 min after adding FeCl3.
- The coagulation process in raw wastewater significantly improved the virus removal from the liquid phase because of adsorption to ferric precipitates and the entrapment during sweep coagulation. Although the surface charge of coagulated flocs was neutralized by ferric precipitates, the virus attachment to coagulated flocs is less than that to suspended solids, probably due to the reduced surface area and adsorption sites.
